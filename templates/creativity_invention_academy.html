Tools inventors actually use

Choose a method, then apply it to something that annoys you in your house, car, phone, neighborhood or community.

1. SCAMPER — improve something that already exists

Pick an object or process and ask: Substitute a part? Combine functions? Adapt an idea from elsewhere? Modify its size or shape? Put it to another use? Eliminate a step? Reverse or rearrange it?

Example: A garage remote. Could you eliminate the need to search for it, or combine it with another safe, accessible control?

2. First principles — challenge the assumptions

Write what the user really needs, then distinguish the requirement from the way it's normally done. Ask: “What absolutely must happen?” and “What could be done differently?”

A person needs to know whether the gate is closed. They don't necessarily need to physically inspect it every time.

3. Inversion — make it worse on purpose

Ask “How could I make this experience terrible?” Write five answers, then flip them into design goals. It's a fast way to find hidden pain points.

4. Borrow from other fields

Look for a solution in nature, logistics, games, manufacturing, accessibility technology, or public transportation that solves a similar type of problem.

5. Design for the broadest useful audience

Ask whether a first-time user can understand it, whether someone with limited mobility or vision can use it, whether it is affordable, and whether it works without special equipment. Not every product fits everyone; choose and test your intended audience.

6. Rapid experiments

Use paper sketches, cardboard, household objects, a phone mockup, or a tiny software prototype. Give someone a task to complete without coaching them. Observe where they hesitate; revise one feature and test again.

Train your observation skills

For the next 24 hours, note three moments when someone has to wait, repeat, search, remember, bend, struggle, or ask for help. Each is a possible design opportunity.

5-minute idea-reset exercise

Quiet attention can help you notice what you've overlooked. Meditation is optional; it isn't a shortcut to inventions.

  1. Minute 1: Sit comfortably, breathe naturally, let distractions pass.
  2. Minute 2: Recall an everyday annoyance without trying to fix it.
  3. Minute 3: Imagine the task becoming effortless. What's different?
  4. Minute 4: Picture the same task from a child's, older adult's, or first-time user's perspective.
  5. Minute 5: Open your eyes and write down three surprising possibilities.

Reflect without self-criticism

After an idea session, answer:

  1. What problem did I understand better?
  2. Which unexpected connection did I make?
  3. What assumption might be wrong?
  4. What would a real user tell me?
  5. What's one small action I can take tomorrow?

The habit: be curious longer than you're critical. Judge ideas only after you've generated several.
